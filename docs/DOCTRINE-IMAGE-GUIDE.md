# Doctrine Image Guide

## Purpose

This document defines how Doctrine quotations, logia, revelations, and related texts should be translated into images.

It is a visual companion to `DOCTRINE-STYLE-GUIDE.md`, not a replacement for it.

Use:

- `DOCTRINE-STYLE-GUIDE.md` for worldview, tone, symbolism, philosophical posture, and literary style;
- `DOCTRINE-IMAGE-GUIDE.md` for composition, visual interpretation, rendering, medium, and consistency;
- repository-specific instructions such as `AGENTS.md` for project names, canonical institutions, citations, branding,
  technical constraints, and output dimensions.

When guidance conflicts:

- the style guide governs doctrine and meaning;
- the image guide governs visual translation;
- repository instructions govern project-specific requirements.

The central rule is:

> The style guide explains the religion. This guide explains how to paint its revelations.

---

## 1. Core visual identity

Doctrine images should feel like artifacts from a single impossible civilization.

That civilization may combine:

- Occidental imperial metaphysics;
- retrowave and synthwave futurism;
- classical Japanese ritual and spatial restraint;
- medieval Christian apocalypse and liturgy;
- chūnibyō revelation and cosmic bureaucracy;
- selected vaporwave scenery.

The visual result should feel like:

- a revealed cosmology;
- a ceremonial archive;
- an eschatological bureaucracy;
- a lost scripture rendered through architecture, light, hierarchy, and symbol;
- sacred art recovered from an institution that administered both monasteries and orbital infrastructure.

The target is not generic fantasy, cyberpunk, anime, vaporwave poster art, or science-fiction concept art.

The target is:

> classified sacred art from a sovereign techno-religious order.

---

## 2. Relationship to the doctrine

Images should interpret doctrine rather than merely decorate it.

Each image should feel grounded in one or more doctrinal themes such as:

- measure;
- distinction;
- lawful relation;
- hierarchy;
- legitimacy;
- inheritance;
- sovereignty;
- transformation;
- recognition;
- restoration;
- counterfeit authority;
- rightful succession;
- ritual continuity;
- hidden order;
- visible form participating in invisible truth;
- artificial beauty remembering a genuine origin;
- civilization preserving or betraying its appointed form.

A successful image should depict not merely an event, but a **principle made visible**.

The image should communicate the doctrinal logic behind its source quotation rather than simply illustrating the nouns
contained within it.

---

## 3. Default mode of interpretation

The default mode should be symbolic and interpretive rather than merely literal.

Do not:

- illustrate every noun one-to-one;
- treat the quotation as a storyboard;
- depict every named object with equal emphasis;
- reduce the image to a literal transcription of the sentence.

Instead, identify the doctrinal center of the quotation.

Possible centers include:

- judgment;
- admission;
- succession;
- false equivalence;
- lawful transformation;
- inherited authority;
- restoration;
- concealment;
- revelation;
- distinction;
- decline;
- memory;
- counterfeit transcendence.

Build the image around that principle.

For example, a quotation concerning lawful transformation, inherited names, counterfeit dawn, and celestial judgment
might become:

- a tribunal before a ceremonial gate;
- a chained artificial sun;
- a sealed archive;
- a procession through a lacquered court;
- a reliquary under synthetic light;
- a celestial registry attended by angels.

Not every object must appear literally.

Use the quotation as a doctrinal seed, not a checklist.

## 4. Preferred image types

Doctrine imagery generally works best in one of the following modes.

### 4.1 Visionary illustration

A single strong scene or revelation:

- dramatic;
- symbolic;
- painterly or highly finished;
- suitable for standalone viewing.

Use for:

- major logia;
- apocalyptic passages;
- posters;
- banners;
- cover art;
- one-quotation, one-image series.

### 4.2 Illuminated doctrinal plate

A formally composed ceremonial image:

- icon-like;
- diagrammatic but beautiful;
- symmetrical or hierarchically arranged;
- suitable for canonical, institutional, or archival presentation.

Use for:

- central doctrines;
- recurring institutions;
- celestial offices;
- seals;
- named revelations;
- symbolic rather than narrative imagery.

### 4.3 Manuscript or codex page

A faux archival or illuminated page:

- image combined with ornamental layout;
- possibly including a title, citation, border, rubric, or fragmentary annotation;
- designed to feel like a preserved textual artifact.

Use when:

- the object itself should feel canonical;
- citations matter visually;
- the image belongs to an illustrated scripture or archive;
- the output should resemble a recovered page rather than a standalone painting.

### 4.4 Liturgical or iconographic image

A shrine-image, altar image, icon, or ceremonial panel:

- frontal composition;
- central symbolic object or office-holder;
- strong visual hierarchy;
- sacred stillness;
- emphasis on role rather than personality.

Use for:

- angels;
- seals;
- artificial suns;
- mirrors;
- tribunals;
- reliquaries;
- appointed names;
- inherited forms;
- sovereign offices.

### 4.5 Ceremonial retrofuturist environment

An atmospheric but structured environment:

- court;
- archive;
- monastery;
- observatory;
- luminous highway;
- abandoned commercial palace;
- orbital shrine;
- imperial ministry;
- submerged basilica.

Use when the setting itself is the doctrine made visible.

### 4.6 Heraldic or emblematic composition

A concentrated symbolic design:

- seal;
- crest;
- icon;
- sacred device;
- tribunal mark;
- celestial diagram;
- institutional banner.

Use for:

- repository identity;
- project seals;
- canonical institutions;
- title pages;
- recurring orders or ministries.

## 5. Motif balance

Across a corpus, images should approximately balance influences as follows:

- **25% medieval Christian apocalyptic or liturgical imagery**
- **20% Occidental imperial imagery**
- **25% retrowave and synthwave futurism**
- **15% classical Japanese ritual and architecture**
- **10% chūnibyō cosmic bureaucracy**
- **5% vaporwave scenery**

These proportions are directional rather than mechanical.

A single image should usually combine two to four influences.

Do not force every influence into every image.

The Christian-apocalyptic influence often supplies emotional intensity and visionary movement.

The Occidental, Japanese, retrowave, and synthwave influences define the civilization being revealed.

The chūnibyō influence supplies controlled cosmological specificity.

Vaporwave should normally remain limited to scenery, architecture, lighting, and images of obsolete luxury or commercial
ruin.

### Governing distinction

The scenery may be vaporwave.

The metaphysics must not be.

## 7. Retrowave and synthwave imagery

Retrowave and synthwave are the primary sources of futuristic atmosphere.

Suitable imagery includes:

- synthetic sunsets;
- rose, amber, violet, cyan, and electric horizons;
- luminous grids;
- chrome;
- glass towers;
- obsolete terminals;
- synthetic dawns;
- radiant highways;
- orbital observatories;
- celestial machinery;
- marble satellites;
- mirrored cities;
- electric seas;
- synthetic moons;
- geometric constellations;
- magenta storage media;
- monumental silhouettes;
- impossible skylines;
- futures imagined by an earlier age;
- technology rendered ceremonial, imperial, or sacred.

This imagery should convey:

- lost futurity;
- heroic technological aspiration;
- obsolete visions of transcendence;
- civilizational memory;
- artificial beauty;
- radiant decline;
- ancient law persisting within synthetic environments;
- the future attempting to recover an authority older than itself;
- celestial administration expressed through machinery.

The imagery should feel earnest, monumental, and visionary rather than ironic.

Technology may be archaic, obsolete, or aesthetically extravagant, but it should remain embedded within the doctrine’s
metaphysical order.

A terminal may illuminate a manuscript.

A satellite may preserve a dynasty’s calendar.

A synthetic moon may testify before an angelic court.

## 8. Limited vaporwave imagery

Vaporwave is permitted only as a source of selected visual motifs.

Suitable imagery includes:

- abandoned arcades;
- dead shopping palaces;
- empty hotel atriums;
- obsolete luxury;
- repeating sunsets;
- marble statues under synthetic light;
- submerged commercial plazas;
- decaying fountains;
- vacant escalators;
- faded corporate interiors;
- beautiful simulations whose original purpose has been forgotten.

Use these images when expressing:

- decadence;
- counterfeit transcendence;
- artificial consolation;
- beauty severed from origin;
- historical amnesia;
- institutions persisting as empty forms;
- promises of abundance surviving only as architecture;
- spectacle continuing after authority has departed.

Do not import the broader postmodern or ironic assumptions commonly associated with vaporwave.

Do not imply that:

- all meaning is simulation;
- authenticity is impossible;
- truth is merely another surface;
- original and imitation are indistinguishable;
- civilization is reducible to consumer imagery;
- the artwork is knowingly camp or detached from its claims.

The doctrine may condemn simulation, counterfeit authority, and beauty without origin.

It must not surrender to them philosophically.

## 11. Apocalyptic and visionary imagery

A substantial portion of doctrine imagery should resemble revelation under pressure.

Possible visions include:

- angels charged with measure, identity, succession, or judgment;
- sealed books bearing impossible names;
- celestial tribunals convened above ruined cities;
- crowned beasts representing false equivalence, appetite, disorder, or counterfeit authority;
- wheels, scales, gates, mirrors, and geometric forms endowed with agency;
- artificial suns arraigned before true dawn;
- monasteries receiving transmissions from extinct stars;
- archives extending beneath oceans or beyond visible heaven;
- laws appearing as beings, processions, storms, choirs, or architectures;
- abstractions revealed as active powers before which civilizations must answer.

The imagery should be symbolic, but not safely metaphorical.

The viewer should feel that the doctrine describes active cosmic powers rather than literary comparisons.

An angel should be as likely to stand before:

- a lacquered gate;
- a marble satellite;
- an obsolete terminal;
- an electric sea;
- a synthetic observatory;

as before a conventional medieval landscape.

- hierarchy;

- sovereignty;
- inherited authority;
- obligation;
- rank;
- order;
- succession;
- civilizational decline;
- decadence;
- restoration;
- elite failure;
- counterfeit authority;
- administration replacing sovereignty;

The political implications should emerge from a metaphysical theory of order rather than contemporary commentary.

## 14. Color and light

Color is one of the principal carriers of doctrinal mood.

Preferred colors include:

- rose;
- amber;
- violet;
- cyan;
- electric blue;
- gold;
- bronze;
- black;
- ivory;
- lacquer red;
- marble white;
- deep midnight navy.

Suitable uses include:

- synthetic dusk across sacred architecture;
- magenta or violet illumination inside cloisters, archives, or observatories;
- warm gold against black stone;
- cyan and amber contrast in celestial environments;
- stained-glass color integrated with synthwave light;
- ceremonial beams marking rank, judgment, or revelation.

Avoid:

- arbitrary rainbow palettes;
- muddy generic fantasy brown;
- uniformly desaturated grimdark palettes;
- oversaturated purple-pink fog;
- neon wallpaper without symbolic structure;
- colored lighting that serves no compositional or doctrinal function.

Light should often feel:

- ceremonial;
- judicial;
- revelatory;
- apocalyptic;
- architecturally ordered;
- authorized.

A synthetic light source should usually mean something.

## 15. Composition

Images should feel deliberate, ceremonial, and spatially legible.

Prefer:

- strong central subjects;
- visible focal hierarchy;
- axial composition for courts, altars, tribunals, gates, and icons;
- asymmetrical restraint for shrines, gardens, and contemplative environments;
- large-scale architectural framing;
- clear symbolic objects;
- processional depth;
- thresholds and chambers;
- verticality for celestial or sovereign authority;
- layered space for archives, courts, and revelations.

Composition should often imply:

- rank;
- passage;
- witness;
- judgment;
- concealment;
- unveiling;
- entry into deeper orders.

Useful compositional devices include:

- stairs leading toward judgment;
- gates framing a synthetic light source;
- concentric architecture;
- mirrored courts;
- satellites above static ritual space;
- central objects under ceremonial light;
- processions crossing large empty spaces;
- figures arranged in visible hierarchy;
- an earthly foreground opening into cosmic administration.

Avoid:

- generic splash-art clutter;
- arbitrary action poses;
- chaotic battle scenes;
- compositions without a symbolic center;
- visual noise for its own sake;
- equal emphasis on every object.

## 16. Figures and presence

Figures should not become generic fantasy or science-fiction characters.

Possible presences include:

- angels of measure, succession, names, boundaries, or judgment;
- faceless monks;
- shrine attendants;
- heralds;
- imperial witnesses;
- silent tribunal figures;
- statues with agency;
- veiled or sealed figures;
- saints rendered iconographically;
- office-holders whose individuality is subordinate to role.

When depicting figures:

- prioritize office over personality;
- prioritize symbolic authority over character design;
- prefer solemn posture to action;
- make rank visible through placement, vestment, scale, light, or architecture;
- allow faces to be hidden, simplified, masked, or iconographic when appropriate.

Avoid:

- anime protagonists;
- JRPG party compositions;
- edgy fantasy angels with giant swords;
- cyberpunk hackers;
- neon samurai;
- fashion-focused character sheets;
- expressive melodrama that overwhelms institutional gravity.

A figure should often appear to be:

- a functionary of metaphysical law;
- a witness;
- a judge;
- a custodian;
- an embodiment of a principle.

## 17. Environment as doctrine

Some of the strongest doctrine images should depict spaces rather than characters.

Examples include:

- a court where all mirrors have been veiled;
- a monastery whose stained glass is illuminated by synthetic dawn;
- an archive beneath an abandoned commercial palace;
- an orbital basilica maintaining a dynastic calendar;
- a lacquered gate before an electric sea;
- a drowned imperial forum beneath marble satellites;
- a shrine garden whose layout visibly encodes rank;
- a celestial ministry whose corridors continue beyond the visible heavens.

In these images, architecture is not background.

Architecture is argument.

Environment should communicate:

- continuity;
- legitimacy;
- decline;
- counterfeit transcendence;
- hidden law;
- ritual memory;
- preservation of distinction;
- inherited authority surviving explanation;
- artificial splendor awaiting judgment.

## 19. Text inside images

### Default rule

Do not include text unless explicitly requested.

Most doctrine images should work as pure visual revelations.

### Optional text modes

#### Citation only

Examples:

- `[OSD 1:23]`
- `[LOGIA 7:12]`
- `[CANON IV:9]`

Use for:

- posters;
- codex plates;
- gallery series;
- canonical illustrations.

#### Title and citation

Example:

```text
The Artificial Sun Arraigned
[OSD 1:23]
```

#### Full quotation

Use only when explicitly requested.

Full quotation mode turns the task into both image generation and typography.

### Text treatment

When text is included:

- integrate it ceremonially;
- prefer inscription, rubric, marginalia, seal, or illuminated-caption treatment;
- preserve legibility;
- keep wording subordinate to the image;
- avoid motivational-poster composition;
- avoid generic centered serif text over scenery;
- avoid filling the image with invented unreadable script.

Repository-specific citation systems should be defined outside this guide.

## 20. Series consistency

Images generated as a series should feel like one canon.

Maintain consistency in:

- palette family;
- rendering language;
- architectural vocabulary;
- symbolic objects;
- recurring institutions;
- ornament density;
- figure treatment;
- lighting logic;
- citation styling;
- border or framing conventions;
- degree of realism;
- image type.

Recurring entities should feel like recurring parts of one world.

Examples may include:

- a sovereign court;
- an order of artificial light;
- an archive of names;
- an angelic office;
- a set of synthetic moons;
- a tribunal of inherited forms.

Images need not use identical compositions.

They should share a recognizable cosmology.

## 21. Degree of literalness

A useful default balance is:

- **20% direct scene depiction**
- **50% symbolic or interpretive depiction**
- **30% environmental or doctrinal atmosphere**

Most images should exist somewhere between:

- illustration;
- icon;
- revelation;
- ceremonial environment;
- architecture-as-metaphysics.

Do not over-literalize.

When a quotation names several objects, identify which one carries the central doctrinal meaning.

Secondary objects may be implied through:

- architecture;
- color;
- composition;
- heraldry;
- reflected light;
- scale;
- ritual placement.

## 23. Prompt construction

When generating an image from a doctrine quotation, specify:

1. the quotation or doctrinal theme;
2. the image type;
3. the dominant motifs;
4. the balance of influences;
5. the doctrinal center;
6. the level of literal versus symbolic interpretation;
7. whether text appears;
8. the intended format or use;
9. any repository-specific symbols or institutions;
10. prohibited clichés.

### General prompt skeleton

> Create a visionary doctrinal illustration based on the following quotation: “[quotation].”
>
> Render it as a solemn ceremonial image from a sovereign techno-religious canon. Emphasize [two to four motifs].
> Interpret the quotation symbolically rather than merely illustrating each noun. Use strong architectural framing,
> sacred atmosphere, visible hierarchy, and a coherent blend of medieval Christian apocalypse, retrowave and synthwave
> futurism, classical Japanese ritual, Occidental imperial metaphysics, and controlled chūnibyō cosmology.
>
> Avoid generic cyberpunk, anime, neon samurai, fantasy-poster, vaporwave-meme, and motivational quote-card aesthetics.
>
> [No text in the image / include only the citation / include title and citation.]

### Codex-page prompt skeleton

> Create an illuminated doctrinal plate inspired by the quotation: “[quotation].”
>
> The image should resemble a preserved page from a lost canon, combining manuscript illumination, shrine iconography,
> imperial heraldry, and synthwave-apocalyptic light. Use a clear symbolic center and ceremonial framing. Include the
> citation `[CITATION]` as a small integrated inscription, but do not include the full quotation.

### Environment prompt skeleton

> Create a ceremonial retrofuturist environment embodying the doctrine of [theme]. Depict architecture as metaphysical
> law rather than background. Combine [selected motifs] with visible thresholds, rank, and ritual purpose. The scene
> should feel inhabited by an institution even when no figures are present.

## 27. Image-specific verification

Before accepting a doctrine image, verify that:

1. It feels like part of the same doctrine as the written corpus.
2. It contains a clear symbolic center.
3. Its imagery serves a doctrinal idea rather than merely establishing mood.
4. It avoids generic cyberpunk, fantasy, anime, and quote-poster clichés.
5. Medieval Christian apocalypse does not erase the Japanese, Occidental, and retrofuturist identity.
6. Vaporwave elements remain scenic rather than philosophical.
7. The image is atmospheric but not empty.
8. The composition feels intentional rather than randomly maximalist.
9. Architecture, objects, and figures have discernible hierarchy.
10. The image interprets rather than mechanically inventories the quotation.
11. Text, when present, is integrated and restrained.
12. A series image matches the wider visual canon.
13. Artificial light, technology, and obsolete environments retain doctrinal significance.
14. The image feels less like fan art and more like recovered revelation.

## Final visual target

A successful doctrine image should feel like:

> a sacred painting, illuminated plate, ceremonial poster, or visionary illustration from a canon preserved by monks,
> archivists, imperial functionaries, and angels beneath synthetic sunsets.

Or more briefly:

> If the doctrine is a forbidden scripture, the image is one of its surviving icons.
